01

Voice Deepfake Detection for Brazilian Portuguese

In progress. Started August 2026.

Problem

Detect synthetic speech in Brazilian Portuguese, including audio that has passed through a phone line.

What I built

A probe on WavLM features that labels clips as real or synthetic, trained on Portuguese deepfake data. I used ffmpeg to simulate phone-line audio (G.711) and test how the probe holds up.

The pipeline is zero persistence and aligned with LGPD: audio is processed and not stored.

2.6%Equal error rate for the WavLM probe
+10.3Points of error added by G.711 phone audio on unseen generators
4Dataset shortcuts found that the model could exploit
2,481Evaluation clips misaligned by a bug I found

What went wrong

My own real WhatsApp voice notes scored as fake. That suggests the probe learned the dataset's recording conditions, not just the deepfakes.

What I learned

Phone audio is the hard case. Passing audio through G.711 raised the error by 10.3 points on generators the model had not seen.

Problems I found

Auditing the data turned up four shortcuts the model could learn instead of the voice itself, and a bug that misaligned 2,481 evaluation clips.

I also showed that a peer-reviewed dataset's published baselines used the wrong sample rate. I reproduced the authors' scores exactly (r = 1.000000), then traced the cause: real and fake clips at their native rates went into a model built for 16 kHz audio.

Stack
Python, PyTorch, WavLM, ffmpeg
02

World Cup Prediction Model

Problem

Predict the 48-team 2026 World Cup from international match results, then check the predictions against what actually happened.

What I built

A match model trained on 49,372 men's international results since 1872, using only information available before kickoff. Elo ratings, recent form, rest days and head-to-head record feed a logistic regression, and a Dixon-Coles goal model predicts scorelines.

The match probabilities drive a Monte Carlo simulator that plays the full tournament 20,000 times.

61%Group-stage results called correctly, 44 of 72
7Exact scorelines in the group stage
256World Cup matches used to choose the model
20,000Simulations of the full tournament

What went wrong

My first 2022 backtest was overconfident about the favorites. The match probabilities were already calibrated, so the problem was the simulation treating each team's rating as exact across seven games.

I fixed it by giving every team a random strength offset in each simulation, tuned on the 2010 to 2022 World Cups.

What I learned

Fit before each 2010 to 2022 World Cup and scored on those 256 matches, no single model beat the Elo backbone. Blending it with the Dixon-Coles goal model beat all of them (log loss 0.9880 vs 0.9961), so that ensemble is the 2026 engine.

Picking champions was different: pure Elo did best there (Figure 2). XGBoost and extra features from goalscorer data did not beat the backbone either.
